1.	Problem	Statement
1.1	Background
Victims	and	complainants	belonging	to	Scheduled	Castes	and
Scheduled	Tribes	who	approach	the	National	Helpline	Against
Atrocities	(14566),	the	Integrated	Portal,	chatbot,	mobile	application,
IVRS,	or	other	digital	platforms	often	experience	severe	emotional
distress	arising	from	caste-based	discrimination,	violence,	rape,	gang
rape,	murder	of	family	members,	social	boycott,	displacement,
threats,	and	prolonged	legal	proceedings.	Presently,	there	is	no
standardized	mechanism	for	assessing	the	psychological	condition	and
vulnerability	of	victims	at	the	time	of	ﬁrst	contact	with	authorities.
1.2	The	Problem
Design	and	develop	an	AI-enabled	Real-Time	Stress	and	Trauma
Assessment	Module	that	can	assess	the	psychological	stress,	trauma,
fear,	anxiety,	and	vulnerability	levels	of	victims/complainants
interacting	through	NHAA	(14566),	the	Integrated	Portal,	chatbot,
IVRS,	mobile	application,	or	any	other	approved	digital	interface.
1.3	Expected	Solution	Requirements
Analyse	voice	interactions,	speech	patterns,	pauses,	pitch
variation,	emotional	indicators,	and	textual	narratives
Use	Natural	Language	Processing	(NLP),	Speech	Analytics,	and
Emotion	AI	to	identify	signs	of	trauma	and	distress
Generate	a	Stress	Vulnerability	Index	(SVI)	on	a	predeﬁned	scale
Categorize	victims	into	Low,	Moderate,	High,	and	Critical	Risk
categories
Detect	indicators	of	severe	trauma,	fear,	depression,	suicidal
ideation,	intimidation,	social	isolation,	and	extreme	vulnerability
Automatically	recommend	counselling,	legal	aid,	medical
assistance,	police	intervention,	witness	protection,	or	emergency
support	based	on	risk	level
Support	multilingual	interactions,	including	major	Indian
languages	and	dialects
Maintain	privacy,	informed	consent,	conﬁdentiality,	and	ethical	AI
standards
1.4	Stakeholders
Department	of	Social	Justice	and	Empowerment;	National	Helpline
Against	Atrocities	(14566);	State	Governments	and	Union	Territories;
District	Administrations;	Counsellors	and	Mental	Health

2.	Proposed	Solution
2.1	Overview	and	Core	Idea
We	propose	a	real-time,	AI-assisted	risk-triage	layer	that	sits	alongside
the	human	helpline	agent	at	every	point	of	contact	–	voice	call,	IVRS,
chatbot,	mobile	app,	or	web	portal	–	and	continuously	analyses	what	a
caller	says	and	how	they	say	it,	surfacing	a	structured,	multi-
dimensional	picture	of	their	psychological	state	as	the	call	happens.
We	are	deliberately	not	building	an	“AI	that	detects	emotions”	or	an
“AI	that	diagnoses	trauma.”	We	are	building	an	AI-assisted	real-time
trauma-and-vulnerability	triage	layer	that	helps	a	helpline	agent
notice	distress	signals	that	could	otherwise	be	missed	during	a	high-
pressure	conversation.	This	assessment	is	distilled	into	a	single,	easy-
to-read	Stress	Vulnerability	Index	(SVI)	on	a	0-100	scale,	exactly	as
the	problem	statement	asks,	while	a	richer	set	of	underlying	risk
signals	–	each	carrying	its	own	conﬁdence	estimate	–	drives	every
recommendation	the	agent	sees.
The	central	design	principle	is	this:	the	AI	recommends;	a	trained
human	always	decides.	At	no	point	does	the	system	autonomously
contact	police,	trigger	witness	protection,	or	take	any	irreversible
action	on	its	own.	This	is	the	core	architectural	choice,	because	in
caste-atrocity	cases	the	“obvious”	escalation	path	can	itself	become	a
source	of	harm	if	the	local	police	or	administration	is	compromised,
and	because	a	wrongly	triggered	irreversible	action	can	cause	more
damage	to	a	victim	than	a	missed	one.
Scope	boundary,	stated	up	front:
	
we	detect	conversational	and
acoustic	indicators	associated	with	elevated	vulnerability;	we	do	not
diagnose	depression,	PTSD,	dissociation,	or	any	other	psychiatric
condition.	Every	dimension	in	Section	2.2	is	an	indicator	surfaced	for
a	trained	human,	never	a	clinical	ﬁnding	asserted	by	the	system.
2.2	Eight	Observable	Risk	Dimensions	–
Full	Coverage	of	the	Required	Indicator
List
Trauma	is	not	one-dimensional,	and	a	system	that	only	tracks	the	two
or	three	most	dramatic	symptoms	will	miss	victims	who	present
diﬀerently.	To	ensure	every	indicator	named	in	the	problem	statement
is	explicitly	and	separately	tracked	–	not	folded	silently	into	something
else	–	we	compute	eight	independent,	evidence-based	risk	dimensions
for	every	caller:
1.	
Acute	distress
	
–	immediate	emotional	overwhelm	(crying,	panic,
breathlessness)
2.	
Depression	indicators
	
–	persistent	low	mood,	hopelessness,
anhedonia	(“nothing	matters	now,”	loss	of	interest	in	previously
important	things),	ﬂattened	or	monotone	vocal	aﬀect,	self-blame
language	–	distinct	from	acute	distress	because	it	reﬂects	a
sustained	state	rather	than	a	momentary	spike
--- PAGE 4 ---
3.	
Self-harm	risk	indicators
	
–	linguistic	and	contextual	signals
potentially	associated	with	suicidal	ideation	or	self-harm	intent,
escalated	for	human	assessment	rather	than	treated	as	a	diagnosis
4.	
Fear	of	retaliation
	
–	signals	that	the	caller	is	afraid	of	future
consequences	from	reporting	(community	backlash,	loss	of
livelihood,	family	pressure	to	withdraw	the	complaint)
5.	
Intimidation	signals
	
–	signals	that	the	caller	is	currently	being
threatened	or	coerced	–	by	the	perpetrator,	their	associates,	or
local	authority	ﬁgures	–	distinct	from	fear	of	retaliation	because	it
reﬂects	an	active,	ongoing	threat	rather	than	an	anticipated	future
one
6.	
Dissociation
	
–	signs	that	the	caller	has	mentally	disconnected
from	the	conversation,	often	from	severe	trauma
7.	
Social	isolation
	
–	indicators	of	being	cut	oﬀ	from	family,
community,	or	support	networks
8.	
Chronic	trauma	indicators
	
–	signs	that	this	is	not	a	ﬁrst
incident,	but	a	pattern
Each	dimension	is	scored	independently	and	reported	as	a	risk	level
plus	a	conﬁdence	estimate	(e.g.,	“Fear	of	retaliation:	High	–	87%
conﬁdence”),	so	the	agent	always	knows	not	just	what	the	system
found	but	how	sure	it	is.

2.3	The	Stress	Vulnerability	Index	(SVI)
The	SVI	is	computed	as	a	documented,	open,	weighted	combination	of
the	eight	dimensions:
SVI	=	w1.acute_distress	+	w2.depression	+	w3.self_harm_risk
				
+	w4.fear_of_retaliation	+	w5.intimidation	+	w6 .dissociation
				
+	w7.social_isolation	+	w8.chronic_markers
where	wi	>=	0	and	the	weights	sum	to	1.
On	the	weights:	for	the	prototype,	the	weights	are	conﬁgurable,
evidence-informed,	and	expert-reviewable	–	not	asserted	as	clinically
validated.	
self_harm_risk
	
and	
intimidation
	
are	given	the	highest
starting	weights,	because	missing	genuine	self-harm	risk	is	the
costliest	possible	error,	and	because	active	intimidation	means	the
caller	may	be	in	immediate	danger	independent	of	what	they	are	able
to	say	on	the	call.	In	production,	these	starting	weights	would	be
reviewed	and	calibrated	by	qualiﬁed	mental-health	professionals	and
--- PAGE 5 ---
domain	authorities	before	deployment,	and	the	weighting	scheme
itself	–	not	just	its	output	–	is	exposed	to	supervisors,	not	hidden
inside	the	model.
This	gives	the	Ministry,	district	administrators,	and	helpline
supervisors	exactly	what	they	asked	for	–	one	number,	on	one	scale,
that	instantly	communicates	urgency	–	while	the	actual	clinical
reasoning	underneath	remains	multi-dimensional,	transparent,	and
auditable.	Nothing	is	hidden	inside	a	black	box:	every	SVI	value	can
be	traced	back	to	exactly	which	of	the	eight	dimensions	drove	it,	and
by	how	much	(see	Section	2.16,	Explainable	AI	Panel).

2.5	How	Recommendations	Work	–
Automatic	in	Presentation,	Human-Gated
in	Eﬀect
The	problem	statement	asks	the	system	to	“automatically	recommend
counselling,	legal	aid,	medical	assistance,	police	intervention,	witness
protection,	or	emergency	support.”	We	satisfy	this	literally,	across	all
six	named	support	types,	and	we	group	them	by	how	reversible	each
one	is:
Beneﬁcial,	low-risk	referrals	–	counselling	and	legal	aid.
Support	the	caller	can	only	beneﬁt	from,	never	be	harmed	by.
Actioned	quickly	once	the	caller	has	been	asked	in	the	moment
whether	they	want	that	support	and	the	agent	conﬁrms.
Medical	assistance	–	split	by	urgency.
	
A	non-urgent	medical-
assistance	referral	(e.g.,	connecting	the	caller	to	a	health	worker	or
clinic	for	after-the-fact	care)	follows	the	same	fast,	agent-conﬁrmed
path	as	counselling	and	legal	aid.	An	urgent	medical	dispatch	(e.g.,
the	caller	or	a	family	member	is	currently	injured	and	needs	an
ambulance)	is	fast-tracked	for	agent	conﬁrmation	without	requiring
the	senior-reviewer	sign-oﬀ	that	gates	police/witness-protection
actions	–	because	in	a	genuine	medical	emergency,	the	cost	of	delay
can	itself	be	life-threatening,	and	dispatching	medical	aid	does	not
carry	the	same	risk	of	institutional	complicity	that	police	involvement
can	carry	in	caste-atrocity	cases.	The	distinction	is	deliberate:
reversibility	and	risk-of-harm-from-the-response-itself,	not	just	“how
serious,”	determines	the	approval	path.
Police	intervention,	witness	protection,	and	non-medical
emergency	escalation.
	
Diﬀicult	or	impossible	to	reverse,	and	in	the
speciﬁc	context	of	caste-based	atrocities,	the	very	institution	being
invoked	can	sometimes	be	complicit	in	the	harm.	These	require	a
second,	distinct	human	–	a	senior	reviewer	–	to	explicitly	approve
before	anything	is	triggered,	enforced	as	a	hard	rule	in	the	decision
logic	so	the	action	can	never	reach	an	executed	state	without	that
sign-oﬀ	having	actually	occurred.
2.6	Analysing	Voice:	What	We	Listen	For,
Beyond	Words
Most	systems	that	attempt	to	detect	distress	from	speech	rely	on
basic	sentiment	analysis	of	words	alone,	or	at	best	a	shallow	read	of
tone	and	pitch.	In	line	with	the	problem	statement’s	call	for	NLP,
Speech	Analytics,	and	Emotion	AI,	we	combine	all	three	as	separate,
complementary	evidence	layers	–	while	being	explicit	that	none	of
--- PAGE 7 ---
them,	alone	or	combined,	lets	the	system	determine	with	certainty
how	a	caller	feels.	They	are	supporting	evidence	for	a	trained	human,
not	a	verdict.
NLP	/	lexical	content
	
–	what	the	caller	actually	says,	analysed	for
direct	and	indirect	disclosures	of	distress,	fear,	depression,
intimidation,	isolation,	or	self-harm	risk,	in	Hindi,	English,	and
naturally	code-switched	Hindi-English	speech,	without	translating	the
caller’s	words	into	English	ﬁrst.
Speech	Analytics	/	acoustic	features
	
–	pitch	variation,	jitter,
shimmer,	energy,	and	speaking	rate,	extracted	directly	from	the	raw
audio.
Emotion	AI	/	paralinguistic	modelling
	
–	industry-standard	aﬀect-
recognition	modelling	applied	to	the	acoustic	layer	(e.g.,	ﬂattened
aﬀect	as	a	depression	indicator,	tremor	or	breathlessness	as	acute-
distress	indicators).	We	use	this	as	a	supporting	signal	to	the	lexical
content,	not	as	the	primary	decision-maker,	because	acoustic-only
emotion	detection	is	known	to	be	unreliable	across	diﬀerent	speakers,
dialects,	and	recording	conditions	–	a	limitation	we	surface	to	the
agent	via	the	conﬁdence	score	(Section	2.17),	not	one	we	paper	over.
Silence	itself,	treated	as	a	signal
	
–	the	component	we	consider
most	distinctive	about	our	approach.	Nearly	every	voice-analysis
system	treats	a	pause	as	noise	to	be	ﬁltered	out.	We	instead	treat
silence	as	a	structured,	meaningful	signal:	unusual	silence	is	treated
as	an	observable	signal	that	may	contribute	to	the	dissociation	and
acute-distress	scores,	not	as	proof	that	dissociation	is	occurring.
Severe	dissociation	can	present	as	speech	simply	stopping	mid-
sentence	for	several	seconds	before	resuming	as	if	nothing	happened,
so	an	unexplained,	grammatically	unmotivated	pause	is	scored	as
evidence	worth	surfacing	to	the	agent	–	one	input	among	several,
never	a	standalone	diagnosis.	The	system	measures	how	long	a
silence	lasts,	precisely	where	it	falls	in	the	sentence,	what	word	came
immediately	before	it,	and	whether	the	pause	overlaps	with	signs	of
crying	or	irregular	breathing.
2.7	Multilingual	Design
Support	for	Indian	languages	and	dialects	is	a	ﬁrst-class	design
requirement,	not	an	add-on.	Rather	than	translating	every	call	into
English	and	running	a	single	English-language	model,	the	system	uses
language-native	processing	that	preserves	the	caller’s	own	words,
dialect,	and	natural	code-switching	between	languages,	so	idioms	of
distress	speciﬁc	to	a	region	or	community	are	recognised	as	they	are
actually	spoken.	This	design	choice	is	directly	supported	by	current
research	into	cross-lingual	mental-health	language	processing	for
Indian	languages,	which	documents	that	expressions	such	as	“man	ka
bhoj”	(a	burden	on	the	mind)	carry	culturally	speciﬁc	meaning	entirely
absent	from	Western	clinical	vocabularies	and	standard	NLP
tokenizers,	and	that	code-mixed	Hindi-English	phrasing	measurably
reduces	the	accuracy	of	systems	that	are	not	built	to	handle	it	directly.
Published	research	on	speech-based	distress	detection	also	shows	that
pause	and	silence	patterns	are	not	uniformly	informative	across	every
language	–	their	predictive	strength	varies.	We	treat	this	as	a	design
constraint	rather	than	an	assumption:	the	Silence	Fingerprint’s
contribution	to	each	risk	dimension	is	calibrated	and	validated
separately	for	each	supported	language,	and	if	a	given	language
--- PAGE 8 ---
shows	the	silence	signal	is	not	reliably	informative	for	that	language’s
speech	patterns,	the	system	relies	more	heavily	on	the	lexical	and
acoustic	signals	for	that	language	rather	than	assuming	a	one-size-
ﬁts-all	silence	model	transfers	cleanly	across	Hindi,	English,	and	code-
switched	speech.
2.8	Consent,	Privacy,	and	Conﬁdentiality
Because	this	system	listens	to	and	analyses	some	of	the	most	sensitive
disclosures	a	person	can	make,	we	treat	consent,	privacy,	and
conﬁdentiality	as	three	related	but	distinct	commitments.
Consent	is	an	ongoing,	revocable	relationship	with	the	caller,	not	a
one-time	checkbox.	Three	separate	permissions	are	tracked
independently:	whether	the	call	may	be	recorded	at	all,	whether	the
AI	may	analyse	it,	and	whether	the	anonymised	data	may	later	be
used	to	improve	the	system.	Consent	is	re-checked	during	the	call
itself,	at	moments	the	system	detects	as	signiﬁcant,	and	this	re-check
is	initiated	by	the	system,	not	the	human	agent,	so	the	emotional
dynamic	between	caller	and	agent	is	never	used	to	extract	consent.	If
the	caller	revokes	consent	at	any	point,	AI	analysis	stops	immediately,
while	the	human	agent’s	ability	to	help	the	caller	continues
uninterrupted.
Conﬁdentiality	is	enforced	as	a	set	of	concrete	controls,	not	just	a
policy	statement:
Encryption
	
of	call	recordings	and	transcripts	at	rest	and	in
transit.
Need-to-know	access	control
	
–	a	case	record	is	visible	only	to
the	agent	who	handled	the	call	and	the	speciﬁc	supervisor
assigned	to	that	case;	there	is	no	general	cross-case	search	or
browsing	of	victim	records.
Audit	logging
	
–	every	access	to	a	caller’s	recording,	transcript,	or
risk	proﬁle	is	logged	with	who	accessed	it	and	when,	so	any
inappropriate	access	is	independently	discoverable	after	the	fact.
Deﬁned	retention	and	deletion
	
–	recordings	and	transcripts	are
retained	only	as	long	as	the	case	requires,	with	a	caller-initiated
deletion	request	honoured	for	any	data	covered	by	the	consent
they	gave.
No	re-identiﬁcation	in	aggregate	views
	
–	the	administrator
analytics	dashboard	(Section	2.19)	shows	only	anonymised,
aggregate	statistics;	individual	victim	identities	are	structurally
excluded	from	that	view,	not	merely	hidden	by	convention.
We	take	this	last	point	further	than	a	general	assurance,	because	it	is
precisely	where	a	comparable	real-world	system	failed.	In	2022,	a
major	crisis-text	helpline	was	found	to	have	shared	conversation	data
–	described	by	the	organisation	as	“anonymised”	–	with	a	for-proﬁt
commercial	partner,	and	privacy	researchers	subsequently	noted	that
data	described	as	anonymised	in	other	contexts	has	repeatedly	been
shown	to	be	re-identiﬁable.	Separately,	the	helpline’s	own	data-ethics
advisory	board	was	not	consulted	before	the	decision	was	made,
despite	existing	on	paper	for	that	exact	purpose.	We	treat	both	of
these	as	speciﬁc,	named	failure	modes	to	design	against,	not	general
cautionary	tales.	First,	on	anonymisation:	aggregate	dashboard
ﬁgures	are	subject	to	a	minimum	group-size	threshold	before	being
displayed	at	all	(a	statistic	covering	fewer	than	a	deﬁned	minimum
number	of	cases	is	suppressed	rather	than	shown),	precisely	to
--- PAGE 9 ---
prevent	a	small,	identiﬁable	group	–	for	instance,	a	speciﬁc	village	or
a	speciﬁc	rare	risk	combination	in	a	speciﬁc	district	–	from	being	re-
identiﬁable	even	in	“aggregate”	form.	Second,	on	oversight:	any
proposal	to	use	caller	data	beyond	the	direct	purpose	of	helping	that
speciﬁc	caller	–	including	the	opt-in	“improve	the	system”	pathway
described	above	–	requires	active,	minuted	sign-oﬀ	from	a	standing
data-ethics	review	body	that	meets	on	a	ﬁxed	schedule	regardless	of
whether	a	proposal	is	pending,	speciﬁcally	so	that	oversight	cannot
quietly	lapse	the	way	it	did	in	the	comparable	real-world	case.	No
secondary	use	of	caller	data	proceeds	on	the	strength	of	a	terms-of-
service	disclosure	alone.
2.9	Human	Oversight	as	a	Layered	Safety
Net,	Not	a	Single	Checkpoint
Three	independent	layers	exist	so	a	single	missed	signal	–	by	the	AI	or
by	a	person	–	does	not	go	unnoticed:	(1)	the	agent	handling	the	call	in
real	time,	who	retains	full	authority	to	override,	modify,	or	ignore	any
AI	suggestion;	(2)	a	scheduled	follow-up	at	72	hours	and	again	at	7
days	for	any	caller	assessed	as	Low	or	Moderate	risk;	and	(3)
supervisory	review	after	the	fact,	where	a	portion	of	Low/Moderate
calls	are	spot-checked	and	every	Critical-risk	call	is	reviewed	the
same	day.
2.10	What	the	System	Explicitly	Does	Not
Do
We	detect	conversational	and	acoustic	indicators	associated	with
elevated	vulnerability;	we	do	not	diagnose	depression,	PTSD,
dissociation,	or	any	other	psychiatric	condition.	The	system	does	not
claim	to	determine	with	certainty	what	a	caller	is	feeling.	It	detects
observable,	describable	signals	–	speciﬁc	words,	speciﬁc	patterns	of
silence,	speciﬁc	vocal	characteristics	–	and	presents	them	to	a	trained
human	as	decision-support	information,	each	paired	with	a	conﬁdence
estimate.	Its	job	is	to	make	sure	a	distress	signal	is	never	missed	by
an	overworked	or	momentarily	distracted	human	agent,	not	to	replace
the	clinical	and	legal	judgment	that	only	a	trained	person	can
exercise.	This	applies	equally	to	all	eight	dimensions:	every	dimension
name	(including	“Depression	indicators”	and	“Dissociation”)	describes
a	pattern	the	system	ﬂags	for	human	review,	not	a	clinical	ﬁnding	the
system	asserts.
2.11	Degradation	Under	Real-World
Conditions
The	system	is	designed	to	fail	safely	rather	than	fail	silently.	If	speech
recognition	becomes	unavailable	mid-call,	the	system	falls	back	to
keypad-based	IVRS	input	and	tells	the	agent	explicitly	that	no
transcript	is	available.	If	voice	analysis	fails,	the	system	continues
scoring	based	on	transcript	content	and	the	agent’s	own	notes.	If
conﬁdence	across	all	signals	drops	too	low	to	be	reliable,	a	visible
“Low	conﬁdence”	uncertainty	indicator	is	raised,	the	threshold	for
suggesting	any	high-stakes	action	is	deliberately	raised,	and	the	call	is
routed	to	a	human	reviewer	rather	than	the	system	guessing.	If	the
connection	drops	entirely,	no	captured	data	is	silently	discarded,	and
--- PAGE 10 ---
processing	resumes	cleanly	on	reconnection.	In	every	failure	scenario:
the	system	never	takes	or	suggests	an	irreversible	external	action
while	it	is	uncertain.
A	distinct	and,	in	practice,	more	common	risk	than	an	outright
component	failure	is	silent	accuracy	drift:	a	system	that	keeps	running
and	keeps	producing	scores,	but	whose	scores	quietly	become	less
reliable	as	real-world	conditions	–	background	noise,	an	unfamiliar
dialect,	a	poor-quality	rural	line	–	diverge	from	the	conditions	it	was
calibrated	on.	Published	research	on	multimodal	distress-detection
pipelines	speciﬁcally	warns	that	this	kind	of	fusion	model	degradation
under	real-world	distribution	shift	is	common	and	easy	to	miss,
precisely	because	nothing	crashes	and	no	error	is	raised.	We	monitor
for	this	directly	rather	than	assuming	it	away:	the	rate	at	which
agents	override	or	contradict	the	system’s	suggested	actions	is
tracked	on	an	ongoing	basis	as	a	proxy	signal,	and	a	sustained	rise	in
override	rate	for	a	speciﬁc	language,	dialect,	or	call-quality	condition
triggers	the	same	fairness-style	review	described	in	Section	2.14,
even	though	nothing	in	the	pipeline	has	technically	“failed.”
2.12	Dataset	and	Validation	Strategy	–
Honesty	About	What	Powers	the	Model
There	is	currently	no	large,	publicly	available	Indian	dataset	of	caste-
atrocity-related	trauma	disclosures,	and	we	do	not	pretend	otherwise.
We	treat	this	as	the	solution’s	single	biggest	open	risk	and	address	it
directly	rather	than	glossing	over	it,	in	three	parts.
What	powers	the	prototype	today.
	
The	system’s	word-and-phrase-
level	detection	is	built	to	be	transparent	and	directly	traceable	–	every
score	can	be	explained	by	pointing	to	the	exact	words	or	silence
pattern	that	caused	it,	rather	than	relying	on	an	opaque	model	trained
on	data	that	does	not	exist.	Where	deeper	language	understanding	is
used	to	catch	indirect	disclosures,	negation,	or	unusual	phrasing,	it	is
calibrated	using	a	small	set	of	realistic	vignettes	developed	together
with	licensed	mental-health	professionals,	used	strictly	to	ﬁne-tune
and	calibrate,	not	to	replace	the	transparent,	rules-based	foundation.
How	we	validate	what	we	have,	honestly.
	
Rather	than	an
unveriﬁable	accuracy	claim,	the	prototype	is	evaluated	against	an
expert-labelled	vignette	test	set	held	out	from	calibration,	and	we
report	the	metrics	that	actually	matter	for	a	safety-critical	triage	tool:
recall	on	the	highest-severity	vignettes	(did	the	system	ever	miss	a
case	an	expert	ﬂagged	as	high-risk?),	false-negative	rate	speciﬁcally
on	self-harm	and	intimidation	vignettes	(the	two	dimensions	where	a
miss	is	most	costly),	and	inter-rater	agreement	between	the	system’s
dimension-level	output	and	the	mental-health	professionals’
independent	labelling	of	the	same	vignette.	We	report	these	numbers
as	they	are,	including	where	they	are	weak,	rather	than	presenting	a
single	polished	accuracy	ﬁgure.
To	make	this	concrete	rather	than	aspirational,	we	anchor	our	target
against	the	published	performance	of	comparable,	already-deployed
crisis-line	machine-learning	systems:	a	recent	peer-reviewed
evaluation	of	a	real	crisis-text-line	suicidality	classiﬁer	reported	a
false-negative	rate	near	17%	and	a	false-positive	rate	near	25%	in
production	use.	Our	stated	goal	for	the	self-harm-risk	and	intimidation
dimensions	–	the	two	where	a	miss	is	most	dangerous	–	is	to	meet	or
--- PAGE 11 ---
improve	on	that	published	false-negative	baseline	during	pilot
validation,	and	we	treat	falling	short	of	it	as	a	reason	to	delay	wider
deployment	of	that	speciﬁc	dimension,	not	a	footnote	to	explain	away.
How	we	close	the	data	gap	over	time,	without	waiting	for	it	to
be	closed	to	be	useful.
	
The	system	is	deliberately	architected	so
that	(a)	it	is	useful	and	safe	on	day	one	using	the	transparent,	rules-
plus-vignette	foundation,	and	(b)	it	improves	in	a	structured	way	as
real	data	becomes	available,	without	ever	requiring	an	architecture
change.	The	path	to	real	data	is	itself	consent-gated	and	ethics-
reviewed:	only	calls	where	a	caller	has	explicitly	opted	in	to
“anonymised	data	may	be	used	to	improve	the	system”	(Section	2.8)
ever	become	training	signal,	aggregation	and	anonymisation	happen
before	any	such	data	leaves	the	helpline’s	systems,	and	mental-health
professionals	remain	in	the	loop	to	review	and	label	before	any
retraining.	Until	that	pipeline	has	produced	a	dataset	large	enough	to
meaningfully	change	the	model’s	behaviour,	the	safety	architecture	is
the	actual	mitigation	for	the	data	gap:	low-conﬁdence	outputs	are
visibly	ﬂagged	(Section	2.17)	and	are	structurally	prevented	from
triggering	high-stakes	actions	(Section	2.5,	Section	2.11)	regardless	of
how	the	underlying	scores	were	produced.	In	other	words,	we	do	not
need	the	dataset	to	exist	yet	for	the	system	to	be	safe	to	deploy	–	we
need	the	human-gating	and	conﬁdence-reporting	to	be	strict,	which
they	already	are.

The	system’s	accuracy	at	correctly	identifying	genuine	risk	is	checked
separately	across	caller	gender,	language	and	dialect,	and	recording

2.15	Why	This	Approach	Is	Better	Than
the	Common	Alternative
The	conventional	approach	treats	this	as	an	emotion-detection
problem:	analyse	tone,	run	a	sentiment	classiﬁer,	produce	a	single
stress	score,	and	pass	it	to	a	rule	engine.	Our	solution	diﬀers	in	three
ways.	First,	we	treat	this	explicitly	as	a	triage	problem	borrowed	from
the	same	logic	that	governs	emergency-room	and	disaster	medicine.
Second,	we	refuse	to	let	a	single	number	make	a	high-stakes	decision
by	itself,	preserving	the	full	nuance	of	an	eight-dimension,	conﬁdence-
scored	assessment	underneath	a	single	headline	ﬁgure.	Third,	we
treat	human	judgment	as	the	irreplaceable	ﬁnal	layer	for	anything
irreversible,	which	directly	protects	against	the	single	greatest	risk	in
this	domain:	a	system	that	is	conﬁdently	wrong	at	exactly	the	moment
a	vulnerable	person’s	safety	depends	on	it	being	right.
2.16	Explainable	AI	Panel
The	agent	screen	never	shows	a	bare	number.	Alongside	the	headline
SVI	(e.g.,	“Critical	–	78/100”),	a	breakdown	panel	shows	each	of	the
eight	dimensions	with	a	colour-coded	level	(e.g.,	Acute	distress:	High;
Depression	indicators:	Moderate;	Self-harm	risk	indicators:	Moderate;
Fear	of	retaliation:	High;	Intimidation	signals:	High;	Dissociation:
Moderate;	Social	isolation:	High;	Chronic	trauma:	Moderate),	plus	a
plain-language	“why”	line	generated	directly	from	the	speciﬁc	signals
that	drove	the	score	–	for	example,	“Long	mid-sentence	pause
detected,”	“Statement	indicating	fear	of	retaliation,”	“Flattened	vocal
aﬀect	consistent	with	depression	indicators.”	This	turns	the	SVI	from
a	number	the	agent	has	to	trust	into	a	number	the	agent	can	inspect.
2.17	Conﬁdence	and	Uncertainty
Reporting
Every	one	of	the	eight	risk	dimensions	is	reported	as	risk	level	+
conﬁdence,	not	risk	level	alone	(e.g.,	“Intimidation	signals:	High	–
87%	conﬁdence,”	“Depression	indicators:	Moderate	–	72%
conﬁdence”).	A	separate,	system-wide	AI	Conﬁdence	indicator	sits
above	the	eight-dimension	panel	(e.g.,	“AI	Conﬁdence:	91%,”	or	“Low
conﬁdence	–	noisy	audio”),	and	whenever	this	drops	below	a	safe
threshold	the	interface	explicitly	states	that	the	AI	recommendation
should	not	be	used	for	high-stakes	action	on	its	own.
Current	research	on	clinical	decision-support	interfaces	shows	that
displaying	uncertainty	is	generally	valuable	for	trust	and	appropriate
reliance,	but	also	that	a	dense,	statistically	detailed	presentation	of
that	uncertainty	can	itself	raise	a	user’s	stress	at	exactly	the	moment
they	most	need	to	think	clearly.	We	take	this	ﬁnding	seriously	in	the
interface	design	rather	than	only	in	the	underlying	model:	the
--- PAGE 13 ---
conﬁdence	indicator	shown	to	the	agent	is	deliberately	simple	–	a
single	percentage	and	a	colour,	not	a	breakdown	of	the	statistical
method	behind	it	–	so	that	communicating	honest	uncertainty	does	not
become	its	own	source	of	pressure	on	an	already-stretched	helpline
agent.

2.19	Administrator	Analytics	Dashboard
Beyond	the	individual	caller,	a	district/state-level	dashboard	presents
anonymised,	aggregate	information	for	supervisors	and

distribution,	average	response	time,	counselling	/	legal-aid	/	medical-

completion	rates	for	the	72-hour/7-day	check-ins,	and	ongoing	false-
positive/false-negative	monitoring	to	support	the	fairness	commitment
in	Section	2.14.	Individual	victim	identities	are	never	exposed	in	this
aggregate	view,	consistent	with	the	conﬁdentiality	controls	in	Section
2.8.
2.20	The	Silent	SOS	–	A	Duress	Signal	for
When	Speaking	Freely	Is	Not	Safe
Every	capability	described	so	far	assumes	the	caller	is	free	to	speak
honestly	about	what	is	happening	to	them.	In	a	meaningful	number	of
real	caste-atrocity	cases,	this	assumption	does	not	hold:	a	victim	may
be	calling	from	inside	the	home	of	the	person	threatening	them,	in	the
presence	of	family	members	who	told	them	not	to	call,	or	under	direct
observation	by	someone	who	would	retaliate	if	the	call’s	true	purpose
were	discovered.	No	system	that	relies	only	on	what	a	caller	says	or
how	they	sound	can	help	someone	who	cannot	safely	say	or	sound
anything	unusual	at	all.	This	is	not	a	gap	named	anywhere	in	the
problem	statement,	and	it	is	not	a	gap	addressed	by	any	comparable
helpline	or	crisis-line	system	we	are	aware	of	–	which	is	precisely	why
we	consider	it	worth	solving.
The	mechanism	is	a	pre-agreed,	silent	duress	code,	conceptually
similar	to	a	bank’s	duress	PIN.	At	the	start	of	any	call	or	IVRS	session,
alongside	the	ordinary	consent	ﬂow,	the	caller	is	told	–	once,	and	only
if	they	choose	to	engage	with	this	feature	–	that	they	may	at	any	point
enter	a	speciﬁc	keypad	sequence	silently,	without	saying	anything	out
--- PAGE 14 ---
loud,	to	indicate	that	they	are	not	able	to	speak	freely.	Entering	this
code	does	not	change	anything	audible	on	the	caller’s	end:	the	call
continues	to	sound	completely	ordinary	to	anyone	who	might	be
listening	nearby.	What	changes	is	entirely	on	the	system	and	agent
side.	The	agent’s	screen	immediately	and	discreetly	indicates	that	a
duress	signal	has	been	received,	and	the	agent’s	script	silently	shifts
to	a	mode	built	for	exactly	this	situation:	closed,	yes-or-no	questions
that	the	caller	can	answer	with	a	single	keypress	rather	than	a	spoken
sentence	(“If	you	are	in	the	same	location	as	the	person	who	harmed
you,	press	1.	If	you	are	safe	to	continue	speaking	normally	in	a	few
minutes,	press	2.”).	The	SVI	and	risk	dimensions	continue	to	update
based	on	whatever	signal	is	available,	and	the	case	is	automatically
ﬂagged	for	the	highest	tier	of	urgency	and	same-day	supervisory
review,	regardless	of	what	the	audible	portion	of	the	call	did	or	did	not
contain.
This	feature	is	included	because	it	directly	serves	the	single	most
vulnerable	subset	of	the	population	this	problem	statement	is	about:
victims	who	are	being	actively	controlled	or	surveilled	by	the	person
who	harmed	them	at	the	exact	moment	they	are	trying	to	seek	help.	It
requires	no	exotic	technology	–	keypad-tone	detection	during	a	live
call	is	a	well-established,	decades-old	capability,	and	silent	duress
signalling	itself	is	a	long-established	security	pattern	used	in	banking
and	physical-security	contexts,	and,	closer	to	this	domain,	at	least	one
domestic-violence	hotline	has	reportedly	used	a	comparable	silent
keypad	signal.	What	has	not	been	done,	as	far	as	we	can	ﬁnd,	is
integrating	this	signal	into	a	continuously-updating,	AI-assisted
trauma-triage	pipeline,	where	the	signal	doesn’t	just	trigger	a	single
alert	but	dynamically	reshapes	the	entire	remainder	of	the	interaction.
We	also	design	speciﬁcally	against	the	reason	similar	mechanisms
have	failed	elsewhere.	A	U.S.	Federal	Trade	Commission	review	of
duress-PIN	mechanisms	at	ATMs	found	they	were	never	meaningfully
adopted	in	practice,	concluding	that	the	operational	cost	and	the	risk
of	false	alarms	outweighed	the	beneﬁt.	We	address	both	failure	modes
directly.	False	alarms	are	reduced	by	requiring	a	speciﬁc,	multi-digit
sequence	deliberately	chosen	to	be	extremely	unlikely	to	be	entered
by	accident	during	ordinary	call	navigation,	rather	than	a	single
keypress.	And	unlike	a	mechanism	that	triggers	an	irreversible
external	dispatch	on	its	own,	a	Silent	SOS	signal	here	does	not	bypass
the	human-gating	architecture	described	throughout	this	document	–
it	raises	the	call’s	priority	and	reshapes	the	agent’s	script,	but	any
subsequent	police	or	witness-protection	action	still	passes	through	the
same	senior-approval	gate	as	every	other	Critical-risk
recommendation	(Section	2.5),	so	a	false	trigger	costs	a	more	cautious
conversation,	not	an	unwarranted	dispatch.
2.21	Tamper-Evident	Audit	Trail	for	Legal
and	Evidentiary	Integrity
The	audit	log	described	throughout	this	document	(Section	2.8)
already	records	every	access	to	a	caller’s	data.	We	extend	this	into	a
tamper-evident	record:	each	audit-log	entry	includes	a	cryptographic
hash	of	the	entry	immediately	before	it,	forming	a	hash	chain	across
the	entire	log	for	a	given	case.	If	any	entry	in	the	chain	were	ever
altered	or	deleted	after	the	fact,	the	hash	chain	from	that	point
forward	would	no	longer	verify	–	making	tampering	mathematically
detectable	rather	than	merely	against	policy.
--- PAGE 15 ---
This	matters	speciﬁcally	in	this	domain	because	a	victim’s	ﬁrst
disclosure,	and	the	system’s	contemporaneous,	timestamped
assessment	of	their	distress	and	vulnerability	at	that	moment,	may
later	become	relevant	to	legal	proceedings	under	the	SC/ST
(Prevention	of	Atrocities)	Act	–	for	instance,	in	establishing	that	a
credible	complaint	and	a	clear	risk	signal	existed	at	ﬁrst	contact,
independent	of	what	happened	administratively	afterward.	A	tamper-
evident	log	means	a	court,	a	review	body,	or	the	caller’s	own	legal
counsel	can	trust	that	the	record	of	what	the	system	detected,	and
when,	has	not	been	quietly	altered	by	anyone	with	system	access	after
the	fact.	This	is	a	comparatively	small	addition	to	what	has	already
been	built,	and	it	converts	the	audit	log	from	an	internal	operations
tool	into	something	that	can	credibly	support	the	victim’s	own	legal
position	later.
2.22	Closing	the	Referral	Black	Hole	–
Real-Time	Availability	and	Warm	Hand-oﬀ
The	most	common	point	of	failure	in	victim	support	systems	generally
is	not	detection	–	it	is	what	happens	immediately	after	a	referral	is
made.	A	victim	told	to	“please	contact	this	counsellor”	or	handed	a
phone	number	for	legal	aid	very	often	never	follows	up,	not	from	lack
of	need	but	because	the	moment	of	willingness	passes,	the	number
goes	unanswered,	or	the	service	is	at	capacity.	This	failure	mode	is
well	documented	across	support	services	generally	and	is	not	speciﬁc
to	this	helpline,	which	is	exactly	why	solving	it	here	is	a	meaningful,
transferable	improvement	rather	than	a	narrow	ﬁx.
When	a	counselling	or	legal-aid	referral	is	conﬁrmed	during	a	call
(Section	2.5),	the	system	checks	real-time	appointment	availability	at
the	nearest	appropriate,	already-empanelled	counselling	centre	or
legal-aid	clinic,	and	oﬀers	to	book	a	speciﬁc	slot	immediately,	live,
while	the	caller	is	still	on	the	line	–	a	warm	hand-oﬀ	with	a	conﬁrmed
appointment,	not	a	name	and	a	number	to	follow	up	on	alone	later.
Where	the	caller	consents,	the	receiving	counsellor	or	legal-aid
worker	is	given	the	case’s	risk-dimension	summary	in	advance
(Section	2.16),	so	the	victim	does	not	have	to	re-explain	their	situation
from	nothing	to	a	second	stranger	immediately	after	having	just
disclosed	it	to	the	ﬁrst.
This	directly	strengthens	the	“who	beneﬁts	and	how	do	we	measure
it”	question	central	to	demonstrating	real	impact:	the	system	does	not
stop	at	generating	a	recommendation,	but	measurably	improves	the
odds	that	the	recommended	support	is	actually	received,	which	is	the
outcome	that	was	always	the	point.
2.23	Agent	Secondary-Trauma	Protection
–	Protecting	the	Protector
Every	feature	described	so	far	protects	the	caller.	This	one	addresses
a	real	and	well-documented	problem	that	the	problem	statement	does
not	ask	about	at	all:	helpline	agents	who	spend	entire	shifts	absorbing
repeated,	severe	distress	disclosures	are	themselves	at	serious	risk	of
vicarious	trauma	and	burnout,	a	documented	occupational	hazard
across	crisis-line	and	helpline	staﬀing	generally.	An	agent	who	is
--- PAGE 16 ---
quietly	burning	out	is	also	a	less	reliable	safety	net	for	the	very
victims	this	system	exists	to	protect,	which	makes	this	a	genuine
extension	of	the	solution’s	own	goals,	not	a	tangential	addition.
We	propose	applying	the	same	acoustic	and	conversational	analysis
techniques	already	built	for	callers	(Section	2.6),	pointed	instead	–
with	the	agent’s	own	informed	consent,	as	a	workplace-wellbeing	tool
they	opt	into,	never	a	covert	performance-monitoring	system	–	at	the
agent’s	own	voice	across	the	course	of	a	shift.	Signs	of	accumulating
strain	(ﬂattening	vocal	aﬀect,	slowing	response	times,	increasingly
short	or	clipped	responses	across	successive	calls)	generate	a	private,
supervisor-visible	signal	suggesting	the	agent	may	beneﬁt	from	a
break,	never	a	score	attached	to	their	name	for	evaluation	purposes,
and	never	something	the	agent	themselves	cannot	see	or	dispute.	The
framing	is	deliberately	protective,	not	evaluative:	this	exists	to	catch
an	agent	quietly	struggling	before	it	aﬀects	their	wellbeing	or	a
caller’s	experience,	in	the	same	spirit	as	the	victim-facing	safety	net
described	in	Section	2.9.
Existing	crisis-line	practice	for	detecting	vicarious	trauma	and
burnout	relies	almost	entirely	on	periodic	self-report	check-ins	and
clinical	supervision,	and	published	reviews	of	this	practice	speciﬁcally
note	that	most	studies	in	this	area	do	not	use	any	validated
measurement	instrument	at	all.	Rather	than	inventing	our	own	ad	hoc
heuristic	for	what	counts	as	“strain,”	we	anchor	the	acoustic	signal
against	an	already-validated,	widely	used	occupational	instrument	for
exactly	this	purpose	–	the	Professional	Quality	of	Life	Scale	(ProQOL),
which	speciﬁcally	measures	compassion	fatigue	and	burnout	in
helping	professions	–	so	the	automated	signal	is	calibrated	against	a
real	clinical	standard	rather	than	a	number	we	invented,	and	so	its
output	can	be	meaningfully	compared	against	the	periodic	self-report
the	agent	would	otherwise	ﬁll	out.
This	reframes	the	system	as	a	whole	from	“a	tool	that	watches
victims”	to	“a	system	that	safeguards	everyone	in	the	chain	of	care”	–
and	we	are	not	aware	of	this	being	addressed	by	any	comparable
helpline	technology,	which	is	precisely	the	kind	of	unclaimed,
genuinely	useful	ground	worth	occupying.
2.24	Accessibility	Bridge	for	Non-Verbal
Disclosure
Everything	described	so	far	is	built	around	a	voice	call.	A	meaningful
population	the	problem	statement’s	own	list	of	channels	already
gestures	toward	–	via	its	inclusion	of	the	chatbot,	mobile	application,
and	portal	–	cannot	or	should	not	rely	on	the	voice	channel	at	all:
callers	who	are	deaf	or	hard	of	hearing,	callers	with	speech-aﬀecting
disabilities,	and	callers	for	whom	typing	quietly	is	the	only	safe	way	to
communicate	in	their	present	circumstances.
We	extend	the	same	eight-dimension	risk	analysis	(Section	2.2)	to	the
text-based	channels	using	exactly	the	lexical	layer	already	built	for
voice	calls	(Section	2.6),	so	a	caller	interacting	purely	through	the
chatbot	or	SMS-based	portal	receives	the	identical	quality	of	trauma-
and-vulnerability	assessment	as	a	caller	on	a	voice	line	–	the	same
SVI,	the	same	explainable	panel,	the	same	human-gated
recommendations	–	rather	than	a	stripped-down,	second-class	version
of	the	system.	This	is	a	comparatively	small	engineering	extension,
since	the	lexical	analysis	layer	is	channel-agnostic	by	design,	but	it
--- PAGE 17 ---
ensures	that	a	population	already	explicitly	included	in	the	problem
statement’s	list	of	approved	interfaces	is	not,	in	practice,	given	a
materially	inferior	version	of	the	protection	this	system	provides.
2.25	The	36-Hour	Build	Plan	–	MVP	First,
Wow-Factors	as	Ordered	Stretch	Goals
This	build	plan	deliberately	separates	two	things	that	are	easy	to	blur
under	time	pressure:	the	MVP	(Section	2.29)	that	must	be	complete
and	stable	on	its	own,	and	every	enhancement	layered	on	top	of	it.
The	sequencing	below	builds	and	hardens	the	MVP	ﬁrst,	in	full,	before
a	single	hour	is	spent	on	any	stretch-goal	feature	–	because	a	working
core	that	honestly	satisﬁes	the	problem	statement	beats	an
impressive-looking	pile	of	half-ﬁnished	features	every	time	a	judge
asks	to	see	something	actually	run.
Hours	0-3	–	Foundation.
	
Finalize	technology	choices,	assign
ownership	of	each	MVP	pipeline	stage,	and	lock	the	exact	data	format
passed	between	components	before	writing	code.
Hours	3-12	–	MVP	core	pipeline,	built	and	tested	in	isolation.
Speech-to-text	with	word-level	timing;	a	simple	duration-based	silence
ﬂag	(not	yet	the	full	placement-classiﬁed	Silence	Fingerprint);	the
eight-dimension	rules-layer	risk	scorer;	SVI	and	bucket	calculation	–
each	built	and	veriﬁed	independently	against	controlled	test	inputs.
Hours	12-20	–	MVP	end-to-end	wiring.
	
Connect	the	veriﬁed
components	into	a	single	live	pipeline	with	a	real	call-style	interface,
live-updating	risk	scores	and	SVI,	a	basic	suggested-actions	display,
the	human-in-the-loop	conﬁrm/override	layer,	and	–	built	and	tested
with	the	same	priority	as	everything	else	in	this	window,	never
deferred	–	the	senior-approval	gate	for	police/witness-
protection/emergency	actions	and	a	single,	clear,	upfront	consent
step.
Hours	20-24	–	MVP	integration	testing.
	
Test	with	messy,	real-
world-like	input:	pauses	in	unexpected	places,	Hindi-English	code-
switching,	background	noise,	a	caller	who	barely	speaks.	By	the	end
of	hour	24,	the	MVP	must	stand	alone	as	a	complete,	demoable	system
that	fully	and	literally	satisﬁes	the	Problem	Statement’s	Expected
Solution	Requirements	(Section	1.3)	–	with	zero	stretch-goal	features
attempted	yet.

2.26	What	the	Prototype	Looks	Like
The	ﬁnished	prototype	is	two	connected	screens,	run	on	two	machines
side	by	side,	mirroring	a	real	helpline	interaction.
The	caller-facing	screen
	
resembles	a	simple,	phone-style	interface:
a	caller-ID-style	header	identifying	the	line	as	NHAA	14566,	a
language	selector	for	Hindi,	English,	or	mixed	Hindi-English,	a	call-
connect	button,	an	active	call	timer,	and	a	live	waveform.	Before	the
call	is	treated	as	connected,	a	short,	plainly	worded	consent	message
is	shown	and	read	aloud,	and	the	caller’s	choices	are	recorded	before
anything	else	happens.	A	brief,	optional	mention	of	the	Silent	SOS
keypad	code	(Section	2.20)	is	included	in	this	same	consent	step,
worded	carefully	so	its	existence	is	available	to	anyone	who	needs	it
without	drawing	attention	to	it.
The	agent-facing	screen
	
is	where	the	system’s	reasoning	becomes
visible.	A	live	transcript	builds	line	by	line	in	the	language	actually
spoken,	without	forced	English	translation.	Beneath	it,	eight
horizontal	bars	represent	the	eight	risk	dimensions,	each	ﬁlling	and
changing	colour	in	real	time,	paired	with	the	conﬁdence	indicator
from	Section	2.17.	Above	these,	the	headline	SVI	is	shown	as	a	large,
colour-coded	ﬁgure	–	green/yellow/orange/red	for
Low/Moderate/High/Critical.	A	distinct	visual	alert	appears	when	the
system	detects	an	unusual,	unexplained	silence,	and	a	separate,
unmistakable	alert	appears	if	a	Silent	SOS	code	is	entered,	silently
switching	the	on-screen	script	to	the	yes/no	duress	format.	The
explainable	“why”	panel	(Section	2.16)	and	the	running	case	timeline
(Section	2.18)	sit	alongside	the	risk	bars.	Below	all	of	this	sits	the
suggested-actions	panel:	counselling,	legal-aid,	and	medical-
assistance	options	the	agent	can	conﬁrm	quickly	once	the	caller
agrees	–	with	a	live,	bookable	appointment	slot	shown	directly	in	the
panel	(Section	2.22)	–	a	fast-tracked	emergency-medical-dispatch
option	for	acute	physical	danger,	and	police-intervention	or	witness-
protection	options	that	remain	visibly	locked,	marked	as	requiring	a
second,	senior	approval,	until	that	sign-oﬀ	actually	happens.
A	separate	administrator	view	(Section	2.19)	demonstrates	the
aggregate	dashboard	for	judges	evaluating	scalability	beyond	a	single
call,	and	a	small	supervisor-only	panel	demonstrates	the	agent-
wellbeing	signal	(Section	2.23)	alongside	it,	framed	explicitly	as	a
wellbeing	safeguard	rather	than	a	performance	metric.
Together,	these	screens	let	anyone	watching	the	prototype	see	the
entire	intended	interaction	unfold	exactly	as	designed:	a	caller	speaks
naturally	in	their	own	language	–	or	signals	silently	if	they	cannot
--- PAGE 19 ---
speak	freely	at	all	–	the	system	listens	and	reasons	across	text,	voice,
and	silence	simultaneously	across	all	eight	required	indicators,	the
risk	picture	builds	visibly	and	honestly	in	front	of	the	agent,	every
referral	is	followed	all	the	way	through	to	a	real,	booked	outcome
rather	than	stopping	at	a	recommendation,	and	any	action	serious
enough	to	be	irreversible	remains	visibly,	provably	gated	behind	a
human	decision	at	every	step	–	AI	recommends;	trained	humans
decide.

2.29	MVP	Scope	–	The	Core	This	Solution
Cannot	Work	Without
Everything	described	in	this	document	up	to	this	point	is	the	complete
solution.	This	section	exists	to	answer	a	diﬀerent,	equally	important
question:	if	time,	resources,	or	a	36-hour	clock	forced	a	cut,	what	is
the	smallest	version	of	this	system	that	still	honestly	and	safely	does

construction:	every	feature	removed	from	it	is	removed	because	it

irreversible	actions,	and	the	existence	of	real	caller	consent	before
analysis	begins.	A	version	of	this	system	without	either	of	those	is	not
a	smaller	version	of	the	solution	–	it	is	a	diﬀerent	and	unacceptable
one.

2.30	On	Novelty:	What	We	Invented	Versus
What	We	Integrated
Before	presenting	further	additions,	it	is	worth	being	precise	about	a
question	worth	asking	of	any	system	in	this	space:	is	any	of	this
actually	new?	The	honest	answer,	stated	plainly	rather	than	left	for	a
judge	to	discover,	is	that	most	of	the	individual	techniques	used
throughout	this	document	are	not	new.	Multi-dimensional	risk	scoring
is	established	in	clinical	psychology.	Silence	as	a	distress	signal	is
established	in	speech-pathology	research.	Duress	codes	are	decades
old.	Human-in-the-loop	review	is	standard	practice	in	serious	clinical
AI.	Automating	a	validated	wellbeing	instrument	is	a	natural	extension
of	existing	occupational-health	tools,	not	an	invention	of	a	new	one.
What	is	genuinely	new	is	the	speciﬁc	combination	and	the	speciﬁc
context:	no	system	identiﬁed	in	Section	2.28’s	review	combines
rigorous,	multi-axis,	explainable	triage	with	a	code-enforced	human
safety	gate,	designed	speciﬁcally	for	a	population	where	the
institution	meant	to	protect	them	can	itself	be	the	danger.	That	is
integration	novelty,	not	invention	novelty,	and	the	distinction	matters	–
claiming	otherwise	invites	a	credibility	failure	the	moment	a
technically	literate	judge	asks	a	direct	question	about	it.
The	four	capabilities	described	below	are	diﬀerent	in	kind	from
everything	before	them	in	this	document:	as	far	as	we	have	been	able
to	determine,	they	do	not	exist,	even	individually,	in	any	comparable
published	system.	They	are	presented	separately,	and	with	the	same
honesty	about	their	limitations	as	everything	else	in	this	document,
because	a	genuinely	new	idea	deserves	more	scrutiny	of	its	risks,	not
less.
2.31	Cross-Call	Pattern	Linkage	–
Community-Level	Early	Warning
Every	system	reviewed	in	Section	2.28,	and	this	solution	as	described
up	to	this	point,	treats	each	call	in	complete	isolation.	No	system	asks
whether	separate,	unconnected	callers	are	independently	describing
the	same	perpetrator,	the	same	location,	or	a	correlated	pattern	of
intimidation	within	the	same	time	window.	A	single	agent	handling
one	call	has	no	way	to	see	this;	a	supervisor	reviewing	calls	one	at	a
time	does	not	either.
We	propose	a	privacy-preserving	pattern-linkage	layer	that	compares
anonymised	case	descriptors	–	never	caller	identities	–	across	calls
within	a	district:	features	such	as	a	named	individual,	a	village	or
locality,	or	a	speciﬁc	method	of	intimidation	described	independently
by	multiple	callers.	When	a	correlation	crosses	a	deﬁned	threshold,	a
supervisor	–	never	the	system	alone,	and	never	automatically	–	is
shown	a	ﬂag:	for	example,	that	three	unrelated	callers	in	the	same
district	within	the	past	week	independently	described	fear	of
retaliation	involving	the	same	named	individual.	This	turns
individually-scoped	triage	into	a	community-level	early-warning
--- PAGE 26 ---
signal,	which	is	a	capability	absent	from	every	system	in	Section
2.28’s	comparison,	because	none	of	them	are	designed	to	look	beyond
the	single	caller	in	front	of	them.
This	capability	carries	real	risk	and	must	be	built	with	a	matching
level	of	caution:	the	descriptors	compared	must	be	limited	to	features
that	cannot	re-identify	a	speciﬁc	caller	on	their	own,	the	matching
threshold	must	be	set	conservatively	to	avoid	false	correlations	that
stigmatise	an	innocent	third	party,	and	any	resulting	action	is	limited
to	raising	a	ﬂag	for	a	human	review,	never	to	any	automated
escalation.	This	is	the	most	operationally	sensitive	of	the	four	new
additions	in	this	section,	and	we	present	it	as	a	capability	requiring
institutional	partnership	and	formal	privacy	review	before	any	pilot	–
not	a	feature	suitable	for	a	36-hour	build.
2.32	AI	as	a	Live	Trauma-Informed
Interview	Coach
Every	capability	described	earlier	in	this	document	has	the	system
observing	and	scoring	what	the	agent	already	does.	A	diﬀerent	kind	of
addition	is	an	AI	that	actively	coaches	the	agent’s	pacing	during	the
call	itself,	grounded	in	established	forensic-interview	science	–	open-
ended	questioning,	tolerance	for	silence,	avoidance	of	leading
phrasing	–	research	that	already	exists	and	is	well	validated	for
eliciting	disclosures	without	re-traumatising	the	person	disclosing
them,	but	which	is	not,	as	far	as	we	have	found,	applied	as	a	real-time,
in-call	coaching	layer	anywhere	in	this	space.
Concretely:	the	moment	the	Silence	Fingerprint	detects	a	likely
dissociative	pause,	a	small,	unobtrusive	on-screen	cue	suggests	to	the
agent	“let	this	silence	continue	–	do	not	ﬁll	it.”	When	the	risk	proﬁle
suggests	the	caller	may	be	describing	something	they	have	not	fully
disclosed,	a	cue	suggests	“ask	an	open-ended	question	here,	not	a
yes/no	one.”	This	shifts	the	AI	from	an	observer	of	the	conversation	to
an	active	collaborator	in	how	the	conversation	itself	is	conducted,
directly	in	service	of	not	re-traumatising	the	caller	through	well-
intentioned	but	poorly-paced	questioning.
The	necessary	guardrail:	coaching	is	always	a	suggestion	the	agent
can	see	and	dismiss,	never	an	instruction	the	agent	must	follow,	and	it
never	overrides	the	agent’s	own	judgement	or	training.	An	agent	who
has	reason	to	deviate	from	a	coaching	prompt	–	because	they	know
something	about	the	speciﬁc	caller	or	situation	the	system	does	not	–
must	be	free	to	do	so	without	friction,	exactly	as	with	every	other
suggestion	this	system	makes.
2.33	Outcome	Feedback	Loop	–	Closing
the	Field’s	Biggest	Blind	Spot
Every	system	identiﬁed	in	our	literature	review,	including	the	most
rigorously	validated	academic	research,	measures	its	own	accuracy
against	expert	labels	applied	after	the	fact	–	never	against	what
actually	happened	to	the	person	afterward.	This	is	the	single	largest
blind	spot	we	found	across	the	entire	ﬁeld:	a	system	can	be	validated
as	matching	a	mental-health	professional’s	judgement	perfectly	and
--- PAGE 27 ---
still	be	systematically	steering	people	toward	support	that	does	not
actually	help	them,	and	nothing	in	the	existing	literature	would	catch
this.
We	propose	closing	this	gap	directly:	with	separate,	explicit	consent
obtained	at	the	time	of	the	original	call,	the	system	checks	back	with
the	caller	some	weeks	later	–	distinct	from	the	72-hour	and	7-day
safety	check-ins	described	in	Section	2.9,	which	exist	to	catch
deterioration,	not	to	evaluate	the	system	–	speciﬁcally	to	ask,	in	a	low-
pressure	way,	whether	the	support	they	received	actually	helped.	That
real-world	outcome,	not	merely	an	expert’s	contemporaneous	label,	is
fed	into	a	human-reviewed	process	for	recalibrating	the	SVI	weighting
scheme	(Section	2.3)	over	time.
The	necessary	guardrails:	this	outcome	check	is	opt-in,	entirely
separate	from	the	safety	check-ins,	and	never	used	to	evaluate	or
penalise	the	agent	who	handled	the	original	call.	No	weight	is	ever
recalibrated	automatically	from	this	feedback	–	every	proposed
adjustment	is	reviewed	by	the	same	qualiﬁed	mental-health
professionals	and	domain	authorities	already	responsible	for	the	SVI
weighting	scheme,	exactly	as	described	in	Section	2.3,	so	that
outcome	data	informs	human	judgement	rather	than	silently
reshaping	the	model	on	its	own.
2.34	Physical	Safety	Bridge	via	the
Community	Health	Worker	Network
Every	safety	net	described	in	this	document	up	to	this	point	–	the
agent,	the	72-hour	and	7-day	check-ins,	supervisor	review	–	is	phone-
based.	This	is	a	hard	ceiling	shared	by	every	crisis-line	system
identiﬁed	in	Section	2.28’s	comparison:	if	a	Critical-risk	caller
becomes	unreachable	by	phone,	none	of	these	systems	have	any	way
to	verify	their	physical	safety.
India	has	an	existing,	extensive	network	of	community	health	workers
–	ASHA	and	Anganwadi	workers	–	already	embedded	in	rural	and
semi-urban	communities	nationwide,	whose	ordinary	duties	already
include	home	visits	for	unrelated	health	purposes.	We	propose	that	a
Critical-risk	case	where	phone	follow-up	fails	or	is	judged	unsafe	can,
with	the	caller’s	prior	consent	and	under	the	same	senior-approval
gate	that	governs	police	and	witness-protection	actions	(Section	2.5),
be	routed	to	the	already-present	local	ASHA	or	Anganwadi	worker	for
a	discreet,	in-person	welfare	check	–	framed	to	anyone	observing	it	as
a	routine	health	visit,	never	announced	as	connected	to	the	original
call.	This	closes	the	phone-only	ceiling	shared	by	every	comparable
system,	using	existing	government	infrastructure	rather	than
proposing	anything	new	to	build	or	fund.
This	is	the	addition	that	requires	the	most	caution	of	the	four,	and	we
say	so	directly.	A	poorly	handled	welfare	visit	risks	alerting	a
perpetrator	that	the	victim	sought	help,	which	could	itself	cause	harm
–	precisely	the	failure	mode	this	entire	document	is	built	to	avoid
elsewhere.	For	this	reason,	this	capability	is	presented	as	a
documented,	high-value	direction	for	institutional	partnership	and
careful	protocol	design	with	the	relevant	state	health	authorities,	not
as	a	feature	to	be	built	or	demonstrated	in	a	hackathon	setting.
2.35	Where	These	Four	Additions	Fit
--- PAGE 28 ---
None	of	the	four	capabilities	in	this	section	belong	in	the	MVP
(Section	2.29.1)	or	the	wow-factor	layer	(Section	2.29.3):	each
requires	either	formal	privacy	review,	institutional	partnership	with	a

exactly	why	they	are	not	yet	ready	to	demonstrate,	is	worth	more	to
this	document’s	credibility	than	quietly	folding	them	in	as	if	they	were
as	build-ready	as	everything	that	precedes	them.
